# A Household Preferences Sheet for Nonmedical Home Care Visits

A household can be familiar to the person who lives there and harder for someone else to understand. A closed bedroom door may mean “please knock,” even when a visit is expected. An offer to help may be welcome with one activity and unwelcome with another.

These details do not necessarily belong on a list of daily needs. They belong in a conversation about how a person wants to spend time in their own home.

A short household preferences sheet keeps those details together. For families using [nonmedical personal assistance services (PAS)](https://pthhs.net/home-care-services), the point is not another assessment. It is to record the person’s choices without letting family assumptions quietly take over.

## Begin with the person

Ask whether the person would find a written sheet useful and whom they want involved in making it. They may prefer to talk while someone else writes, add notes themselves, or leave some topics off the page.

The Administration for Community Living describes person-centered planning as directed by the person receiving support, with attention to their strengths, preferences, goals, culture, and language. That principle offers a starting point here: a household sheet should reflect the person’s choices, not simply the family’s idea of an efficient visit. [Read ACL’s explanation of person-centered planning](https://acl.gov/programs/consumer-control/person-centered-planning).

Try a question that leaves room for an unexpected answer: “What would you want someone to understand about being here?” A favorite routine may emerge before any practical instruction does.

## Write down what should remain theirs

Families often notice where help could be useful. A preferences sheet should also capture what the person enjoys doing independently.

For example, these hypothetical notes describe preferences rather than actual client information: “I like to choose my clothes myself” communicates something different from “help with getting dressed.” “Please ask before straightening my desk” explains that an apparently untidy space may have an arrangement its owner understands.

Keep the wording specific without turning it into a test of independence. Record a preference, not a claim about what someone can always do. Someone might enjoy setting the table on some days and welcome an offer of help on others.

Distinguish a strong preference from a flexible habit. “I usually have breakfast after reading” leaves room for an ordinary change of plans. “Please ask before moving my books” expresses a boundary.

## Make communication and privacy visible

Ask how the person would like someone to offer help, whether they enjoy chatting during household activities, and when they prefer quiet. Avoid assuming that a relative’s way of communicating suits them.

In guidance written for clinicians, the National Institute on Aging recommends addressing older patients as fellow adults, asking their preferred name, avoiding interruptions, and keeping them involved rather than speaking only with a companion. Applied narrowly to this nonmedical household conversation, the reminder is to speak with the person, not around them. [See NIA’s guidance on talking with older patients](https://www.nia.nih.gov/health/understanding-older-patients).

Privacy preferences deserve plain wording, too: knock before entering a room, ask before opening a cupboard, or leave mail untouched. Leave passwords and unnecessary personal information off the sheet.

## A simple blank sheet

This original template is an **optional family conversation aid**, not an approved PTHHS procedure. It does not replace the provider’s agreed instructions or service plan. Copy this section onto one page, using only the details the person wants recorded.

## My Household Preferences

**Name I prefer:** __________________

**Discussed on:** __________________

**People I want included in this conversation:** __________________________

**Things I enjoy doing for myself:** ____________________________________

**Routines I would like someone to understand:** _________________________

**Which routines are flexible:** ________________________________________

**Please ask before helping me with:** __________________________________

**How I prefer someone to offer help or explain something:** _______________

**Language or quiet time preferences:** __________________________________

**My preferences about rooms, belongings, mail, or visitors:** ______________

**Something I would like done differently:** ______________________________

**Questions to discuss with the provider:** ______________________________

**Where to keep this sheet and whom I want to see it:** ____________________

**When I would like to review it:** ______________________________________

## Keep the current version useful

A preferences sheet is useful only while it sounds like the person it describes. After a visit, a gentle question may be enough: “Was there anything you wanted done differently?” There is no need to complete every blank or turn each visit into a review meeting.

Ask before revising an entry, date the change, and replace outdated household copies of this optional sheet wherever it is kept. Do not use it to revise agency records or instructions. If others have a copy, tell them which version is current. Do not silently change a shared sheet because one family member prefers a different routine.

If a new preference affects visit arrangements, discuss it with the provider rather than assuming that writing it down changes the agreement. Use the [PTHHS contact page](https://pthhs.net/home-care-contact-us) to find the appropriate next step; do not send sensitive health information through ordinary email or social media.

For questions about starting services, see the separate [first-call checklist](https://pthhs.net/articles/home-care-first-call-checklist-houston). The preferences sheet has a different job: keeping the person’s own words and everyday choices at the center of conversations with family and [Primetime Home Health Services](https://pthhs.net).
